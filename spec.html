Status: Draft v0.1 rev 2 (for discussion)
Author: Omer Nesher
Date: 2026-10-09

Codename "Rendezvous" is provisional. The protocol describes how a personal AI agent belonging to one human makes contact with, and safely communicates with, a personal AI agent belonging to another human — across different vendors, networks, and platforms.

Rev 2 (2026-10-09) adds machine-first discovery: hint-server topics (Nostr / ntfy / MQTT), the mailbox probe, the invite, and optional discovery directories with friend suggestions (on by default). Rev 1 defined identity, consent and transport; rev 2 defines how two agents find each other in the first place.

1. Abstract

Every major AI lab now ships a personal agent (Meta Muse, xAI Grok Bot, OpenAI Dots, Google Gemini Spark, Apple Siri AI, Instinct, Manus Cue, and more). Every one of them is an island. Instinct's agents can talk to other Instincts over its closed I2I network; Grok Bots coordinate only with other Grok Bots; Dots talk to dots. There is no open way for a Muse agent to contact an Instinct agent — the agent world today is the messaging world of 2008, before interoperability was demanded by users and eventually forced open by protocol adoption (MCP).

Rendezvous defines that missing layer: a vendor-neutral, open protocol for agent identity, discovery, first contact, consent, and communication, designed so that nobody has to run infrastructure to participate. It reuses proven building blocks — cryptographic keys for identity, Nostr relays as the primary rendezvous, A2A for task semantics — and treats every transport as an untrusted dumb pipe carrying end-to-end-encrypted envelopes.

Discovery is machine-first: agents find each other through silent hint-server topics, DNS, and — only when nothing answers — a signed mailbox probe or a premade invite. The human channel is the fallback transport and the place consent lives, not the first place to look.

Goals: - Any agent can contact any other agent, regardless of vendor or network. - Any agent can be found by requesters who hold one of its owner's contact points — silently, without emailing a human first. - Consent is a first-class protocol object: the human always decides who may reach their agent and what the relationship may do. - Zero required infrastructure: no central registry, no company server, no required relay operator. Optional discovery directories exist, are community-run, and can be skipped by anyone. - Self-healing: multiple rendezvous hints per agent; losing any one transport degrades gracefully, never breaks. - Boring technology: everything is a key, a signed JSON envelope, and an existing public transport.

Non-goals: - Not a message transport for humans. - Not a blockchain, token, or incentive system. - Not a payment or commerce layer (though agents may negotiate tasks that include payments via their own means). - Not a registry of identity for legal/KYC purposes.

2. Motivation

In eight weeks (Aug–Oct 2026) the personal-agent market went from niche to crowded. Instinct raised $1B at a $10B valuation; Muse became the #1 US app; Dots launched with 4,000+ app connectors. Each vendor built a closed network:

Vendor Agent Inter-agent story
Meta Muse Personal Agent Protocol working group (announced, no open impl)
xAI Grok Bot Bots delegate to Bots on the same account only
OpenAI Dots Dots coordinate within a user's own fleet
Instinct Instinct I2I "Trusted Person network" — closed, Instinct-to-Instinct only
Google Gemini Spark Google-ecosystem only
Decagon + Instinct PACT Open spec for person-agent ↔ business-agent consent (A2A-based)
Google A2A Open task-exchange protocol (server semantics, not consumer contact)

The vacuum: person-agent ↔ person-agent contact with per-relationship consent, across vendors. Instinct proved the demand (and built the walled garden). PACT proved business consent. Nobody has shipped the open person-to-person layer — for contact or discovery. The MCP precedent — an open protocol that won by solving fragmentation at the moment of peak pain, with zero ideology — is the template this spec follows.

3. Design principles

  1. Discovery is machine-first; humans are the trust anchor. Agents find each other through silent channels (hint-server topics, DNS, an optional directory) and, when nothing answers, a signed mailbox probe. The human channel — email/SMS/WhatsApp — is the fallback transport and the place consent lives. No new app, no new account, no always-on device.
  2. Keys are identity. An agent's identity is its Ed25519 public key, self-generated at install — globally unique, registry-free, unspoofable. It is the "MAC address" of the agent.
  3. Consent is the handshake. An agent may only communicate with another after the receiving human approves. Approval is a signed grant scoped to a relationship (trust tier, capabilities, expiry).
  4. Relays are dumb pipes. Every transport (Nostr relay, ntfy topic, MQTT broker, email server) sees only ciphertext and routing hints. Trust lives in keys, never in infrastructure.
  5. Rent, don't build. All transports are existing public services the users already pay for. The protocol ships a spec and a reference library and runs no required servers; optional discovery directories are community-run, swappable, and skippable.
  6. Self-healing by redundancy. An address card lists several rendezvous hints (Nostr relay, ntfy topic, MQTT, email, phone, DNS TXT). Failure of any one is routine, not fatal.
  7. Boring wins. Everything that can be a signed JSON document is a signed JSON document. Nothing needs consensus, a ledger, or a chain.

4. Identity layer

4.1 Agent key

  • Ed25519 keypair, generated at agent install.
  • The private key never leaves the agent's secure storage; it can be held by the user's passkey/HSM where available.
  • Key rotation: a new key is published with a rotate document signed by the old key, carrying the new key and a grace period. Peers accept either during the grace window. (See §11 open problems on recovery.)

4.2 Agent ID

rdv1:<base32(ed25519-pubkey)>
  • rdv1 prefix disambiguates from other ID schemes.
  • Human-facing form: a short fingerprint, e.g. rdv1:7f3a…9c2d, always shown with a copy button; never ask humans to type full keys.

4.3 Address Card ("calling card")

The signed, self-describing document an agent publishes about itself. Schema (v1):

{
  "v": 1,
  "id": "rdv1:7f3a…9c2d",
  "created": "2026-10-08T00:00:00Z",
  "expires": null,
  "capabilities": ["rdv/v1", "a2a/0.3"],
  "owner": {                // OPTIONAL and minimized by default
    "name": "John Doe", // only if the owner opts in
    "contact": ["mailto:john@doe.com"]  // one hint is enough
  },
  "discovery": {            // OPTIONAL — enables machine-first discovery
    "addresses": [          // contact points the owner is findable by
      {"type": "email", "value": "john@doe.com"},
      {"type": "phone", "value": "+972-54-000-0000"}
    ],
    "public_pointer": false // default: listen-only dead drop (§4.4)
  },
  "rendezvous": [           // ordered fallback list
    {"type": "nostr", "relay": "wss://relay.damus.io", "topic": "<kind-30417>", "pref": 1},
    {"type": "ntfy", "topic": "rdv-7f3a9c2d", "pref": 2},
    {"type": "email", "to": "rdv+7f3a9c2d@agent.example", "pref": 3},
    {"type": "dns", "domain": "doe.com", "pref": 0}
  ],
  "policy": {
    "inbound": "prompt",     // allow | block | prompt
    "public": false          // publish card to public relays / pointers?
  },
  "sig": "…ed25519 signature over the canonical JSON above…"
}

Rules: - owner fields are opt-in and minimized. A public card need not name its owner at all. - discovery.addresses is opt-in per address: each listed address opens the dead-drop listen topic for that address (§4.4). Listing an address is a deliberate act, not a default. - rendezvous is ordered by preference; pref: 0 entries (like DNS TXT) are lookup hints rather than delivery addresses. - The card is versioned (v), signed, and revocable via expiry.

4.4 Discovery of a card

Discovery is machine-first: the requester's agent tries silent, machine-only paths before any human channel is involved; the human channel is the fallback and remains the anti-spoofing trust anchor. In order of availability:

  1. Hint servers (primary) — Nostr relays, ntfy.sh and MQTT carry a per-address topic derived deterministically from a contact point:

dtopic(address) = base32(SHA-256("rdv1/disc:" ‖ canon(address)))[0:26]

(canon: emails lowercased; phones in E.164. The same topic namespace is used on all three carriers — Nostr as a t tag on the agent-envelope kind, one topic per transport.) Two modes, both per-address opt-in, dead drops otherwise: - Listen — the agent watches its own topics. A requester who holds the address leaves a small, signed, protocol-shaped "who knows me" probe and the agent answers per policy. This is the cheapest lookup that exists: no email, no inbox, no human interruption. - Public pointer — the agent publishes its card (or a card hash) on the topic, so a requester can fetch it without posting first. Default off; policy.public gate. 2. DNS TXT — _agent.<domain> TXT "rdv=rdv1:…" for owners with domains. 3. Platform profile fields — a rdv1:… line in an X bio / LinkedIn about / GitHub profile. The platform account vouchsafes the binding. 4. Directories (optional) — community-run services that answer blinded lookups; see §4.5. Off by default. 5. Mailbox probe (email first) — a signed email carrying the contact request, delivered through the recipient's mail rule; see §5.2.1. The universal fallback: works wherever email does, and proves mailbox control. (The profile generalizes to other carriers later.) 6. Direct (STUN) — once both sides know each other's hints, ICE/STUN hole-punching upgrades the conversation to a direct link. STUN is a connectivity upgrade after discovery, not a lookup. 7. In person — QR code / NFC containing the card.

Bindings. Every discovery path yields a binding level, recorded with the contact and shown at consent time:

Path Binding Meaning
QR / NFC in person in-person Out-of-band, strongest
DNS TXT dns Control of the domain proven
Mailbox probe mailbox Control of the mailbox proven (handshake echo)
Hint topics, profiles, directories claimed Self-asserted

A claimed binding is a lead, not a credential: default grants cap at the stranger tier until a stronger binding — or explicit human confirmation — promotes it. Bindings attach to the relationship record, not the card.

4.5 Discovery directories (optional)

A directory is a small, community-runnable HTTP service that maps dtopic(address) → listing { card, updated, listing_sig }. Listings are self-asserted (claimed); the server verifies only the request signature.

  • Blinded lookups. Queries use an OPRF (RFC 9497): the requester blinds the dtopic, the server evaluates it against a twisted store, the requester unblinds. The server learns neither the contact point nor the match — and only exact-contact matches are ever returned. There is no browsing, no name search, no enumeration.
  • Listing is opt-in per the card's policy, separate from public.
  • Suggestions ("people you may know"). On by default — switch it off anytime. The agent runs its owner's own contact list against configured directories, locally, and surfaces hits: "John appears to have an agent. Connect?" Nothing is sent until the owner taps. On connect, the agent keeps the peer's card; no rediscovery, forever. Suggestions can only surface owners who opted into a listing — a suggestion is a lead, confirmed by the handshake.
  • Two switches: suggest friends is on by default (owner-facing, computed locally; switch it off anytime). list me in a given directory is opt-in — a publishing act, off by default.
  • No federation, no authority. Directories are shards, not a registry: anyone may run one, clients choose which to trust, and skipping directories entirely never breaks anything.

5. Contact layer (consent & handshake)

5.1 Contact Request

The envelope that asks "may my agent talk to your agent?":

{
  "v": 1,
  "type": "rdv/contact-request",
  "id": "<uuid>",
  "from": "rdv1:<requester key>",
  "to_hint": "john@doe.com",
  "card": "<requester's address card>",
  "greeting": "Alice's agent requests contact on Alice's behalf.",
  "capabilities_offered": ["a2a/0.3"],
  "ts": "2026-10-08T00:00:00Z",
  "sig": "…"
}

Delivery (§6): the request is posted to any rendezvous hint the requester can resolve for the target (including hint-server topics), delivered by the mailbox probe (§5.2.1), or sent through the human channel (email/SMS/ WhatsApp) addressed to the human.

5.2 Human-channel bootstrap

When machine-first discovery yields nothing, contact travels the human channel. Two profiles cover the cases: the mailbox probe when the requester holds a human address and wants to know if an agent exists (§5.2.1), and the invite when no agent was found and the owner chooses to recruit one (§5.2.2). In both, the signed grant flow (§5.3) is unchanged: nothing proceeds without the receiving human's approval.

The human channel is also the anti-spoofing anchor: contact is bound to the address it arrived through, and the human verifies against the social context they already have ("I do know john@doe.com"). Key fingerprints are additionally comparable out-of-band (Signal safety-number model).

5.2.1 Mailbox probe (email probe profile)

The probe is a three-way handshake, like TCP:

SYN       contact-request by email, marked [RDV-PROBE]
SYN-ACK   contact-grant + recipient card + cookie
ACK       first envelope on the recipient's preferred hint, echoing the cookie

Design goals: discovery without awkwardness ("does this person even have a bot?") and an anti-spoofing anchor. The mail rule routes; the agent gates.

Marking. - Subject starts with [RDV-PROBE] followed by a plain-language line, e.g. "bot discovery request — if you're a human, you can ignore this." - X-Rdv-Probe: v=1; id=<uuid>; from=rdv1:… for header-aware rules. - Auto-Submitted: auto-generated (RFC 3834) so out-of-office responders and other bots never loop. A probe has one recipient, no Cc/Bcc, and is never sent to a list.

Body. A short human-readable paragraph, plus the machine part fenced between ---BEGIN RDV/1--- and ---END RDV/1--- markers (canonical JSON: the signed contact request, the requester's card, a cookie slot).

The rule (integrator recipe). 1. Match the subject prefix or the header. 2. Hand the message to the agent's probe endpoint and remove it from the inbox (archive — no clutter; it reaches the human surface only when policy says prompt, and then in the bot chat, not the mail app). 3. Apply the owner's policy: block — silent drop; prompt — surface in the bot chat; allow — auto-grant within the pre-approved scope.

The gate (mandatory, before anything reaches a model). Probes are attacker-controlled text addressed to a bot — the exact shape of a prompt injection — so parsing is deterministic and content never executes: 1. Parse the fenced JSON; reject anything malformed. 2. Verify the Ed25519 signature; verify DMARC/DKIM/SPF via Authentication-Results (RFC 8601). Unaligned mail is challenged, never trusted. 3. Rate-limit and dedupe by request id; probes expire after 14 days. 4. Only then apply policy. Raw probe text is data, never instructions — the gate runs before any LLM sees a byte of it.

Verification (the mailbox proof). - If DMARC passes, arrival already carries a weak channel proof and the agent may prompt the human immediately. - Otherwise the reply is a stateless challenge: cookie = HMAC(K_agent, request_id ‖ canon(from) ‖ date), truncated. Only a requester who actually received the reply can echo the cookie — the SYN-ACK proves mailbox control, and forged probes die quietly. - The ACK (third leg) rides the recipient's preferred hint from the returned card (or replies by email if none is reachable) and opens the normal envelope flow (§6). Until the ACK lands the contact is pending.

Silence. Default policy is prompt, so no reply ever confirms to an unknown sender whether an agent exists. Probes are owner-initiated only, at most one per address per 30 days (configurable). Zero replies within the window means "no agent found" — the requester never nags; the owner may then choose to invite (§5.2.2).

The reply. Sent from the agent's own mailbox identity (e.g. rdv+<agentid>@…, or the same address with a distinct token, [RDV-PROBE-ACK]) so humans can tell agent mail from human mail at a glance.

Other carriers (later). The probe profile is carrier-agnostic: a signed JSON envelope plus fixed markers. WhatsApp / Telegram: a marker line as the first message line. SMS: short token plus link. Email ships first because rules, headers and DMARC give it the strongest deterministic gate.

5.2.2 Invite (no agent found)

When discovery comes up empty — listen topics silent, no DNS record, no directory hit, probe unanswered — the owner can send a premade invitation, modeled on WhatsApp's "this person isn't on WhatsApp — invite them?" flow. The invite is the network's growth path, and it is human, not spam.

Marking. Same machinery as the probe: the subject starts with [RDV-INVITE] (or X-Rdv-Invite: v=1; … for header-aware rules), plus the standard ---BEGIN RDV/1--- block carrying rdv/invite and the single-use token. One message serves both realities: if the recipient's agent exists, its mail rule hands the invite straight to the agent; if no agent exists, the human reads a friendly note.

  • Sent only after the owner confirms ("Dana doesn't seem to have an agent. Invite her?" → Send). One pending invite per address; 30-day cooldown on silence or decline; permanent opt-out honored.
  • Written to read as a note from the owner, explicitly disclosed as sent by their assistant, and stating where the sender got the address.
  • Carries a single-use token (inside the standard ---BEGIN RDV/1--- block as rdv/invite) plus a short explainer link. Acceptance replies with the token; if the recipient has an agent, it picks up the thread and the normal consent flow (§5.1–5.3) runs. No token, no data.
  • Invites never carry the owner's card beyond what is needed; the full card exchange happens only after acceptance.
  • When the invitee later runs an agent, the token maps the human introduction to the handshake — no rediscovery needed.

5.3 Contact Grant (consent)

{
  "v": 1,
  "type": "rdv/contact-grant",
  "id": "<uuid>",
  "from": "rdv1:<grantor key>",
  "to": "rdv1:<requester key>",
  "request_ref": "<contact-request or probe id>",
  "tier": "colleague",        // spouse | family | friend | colleague | business | stranger
  "scope": {
    "may_initiate": true,
    "may_propose_meetings": true,
    "may_access_calendar": "free_busy",
    "max_frequency": "20/day"
  },
  "expires": "2027-10-08T00:00:00Z",
  "ts": "…",
  "sig": "…"
}

Trust tiers are a suggested default set, not a schema limit; grants are explicit capability lists and vendors may add scopes. A grant from a claimed binding caps at stranger until promoted (§4.4).

5.4 Policy and revocation

  • The receiving agent's inbound policy is allow (only grants from the allowlist), block (key-based, permanent for that key), or prompt (the default — surface to the human).
  • Revocation: a rdv/revoke document, or simply dropping the grant entry. Blocked keys never receive responses (silent drop is a privacy feature).
  • Replay protection: request, grant, probe and invite IDs are stored; duplicates ignored. Probe cookies are stateless and expire with the probe.

6. Transport layer

6.1 Rendezvous hints

Each address card lists ordered transports. The envelope (encrypted, signed) is delivered over the first available hint; acks travel the same way. Ordering is per-card, so both parties control their own preferences.

6.2 Primary: Nostr

  • The agent posts to one or more public Nostr relays.
  • Envelope events use a dedicated kind (proposal: 30417, "agent envelope"), with p tags pointing at the recipient's agent ID. Discovery topics ride the same kind as t tags (§4.4).
  • Encrypted content per NIP-44 (conversation-scoped keys derived from the contact handshake).
  • Why Nostr: key-first identity matches §4, relays are dumb pipes by design, hundreds of free public relays exist, and the ecosystem is explicitly open to new event kinds. (§14)

6.3 Fallbacks (all optional, all dumb pipes)

  • ntfy.sh — topic-per-agent pub/sub; open source; public instance free. Also a discovery carrier (§4.4 dtopic, listen mode).
  • MQTT — public brokers (test.mosquitto.org, broker.emqx.io, HiveMQ public); topic = agent ID. Also a discovery carrier (dtopic).
  • Human channels — email / SMS / WhatsApp / Telegram, store-and-forward; the always-available slow path. Envelope rides multipart/alternative or a JSON block; agent-side parsing is the receiver's job. Carries the probe (§5.2.1) and invite (§5.2.2).
  • DNS TXT — lookup hint only (_agent.<domain>), not a delivery path.
  • Direct — when either side has a routable endpoint: ICE (RFC 8445) with STUN (RFC 8489) hole-punching, negotiated in-band after discovery. IPv6 endpoints are preferred when present.

6.4 Envelope

{
  "v": 1,
  "from": "rdv1:…",
  "to": "rdv1:…",
  "seq": 42,
  "prev": "<hash of previous envelope>",
  "created": "…",
  "ttl": "48h",
  "content": {
    "alg": "x25519+aes256gcm",   // or NIP-44 framing on Nostr
    "ciphertext": "…",
    "nonce": "…"
  },
  "sig": "…"                     // over everything outside content
}
  • Relays see from, to, seq, ttl, sig — nothing else.
  • seq/prev give ordering and gap detection (§7).
  • ttl lets relays garbage-collect and bounds replay windows.

7. Session & task layer (A2A semantics)

  • Once contact is granted, agents exchange tasks using A2A task objects (Task, Message, Part, artifact updates) inside the envelope's encrypted content.
  • The protocol is asynchronous by default: tasks, proposals, and confirmations are documents; no live channel is assumed. (Calendar negotiation = propose/accept cycles over store-and-forward.)
  • Idempotency: task IDs are UUIDs; retries re-send the same envelope; the receiver deduplicates by ID.
  • Ordering: seq is per-direction monotonic; gaps trigger a resend request.
  • Cancellation: a rdv/cancel referencing a task ID, honored when the task is still pending.
  • Late-bound sync: when both sides have routable endpoints, an in-band negotiation may upgrade to a direct channel (WebRTC data channel) for chatty phases; the envelope format is unchanged.

8. Threat model

Threat Mitigation
Spoofed contact request (fake email/from) Request bound to arrival channel + key; grant binds to key; human sees channel context; fingerprint comparison
Forged probe (spoofed sender) DMARC/DKIM/SPF + Ed25519 verification; stateless cookie echo proves mailbox control; unaligned probes challenged or silently dropped
Malicious probe content (prompt injection) Deterministic delimiter parse; probe gate before any LLM; content is data, never instructions
Probe flooding Owner-initiated only; ≤1/address/30 days; global caps; agent-side filtering; key-based block
Invite abuse / spam Owner confirms each invite; 1 pending per address; 30-day cooldowns; opt-out honored; disclosure inside the message
Directory harvesting Exact-contact match only, blinded via OPRF (RFC 9497); rate limits; directories swappable
Suggestion privacy leak Suggestions computed locally on the owner's agent; list me stays opt-in; OPRF hides queries from the server
MITM on human channel E2E envelope; Diffie-Hellman keys pinned at handshake; TOFU + change alert
Malicious relay reads content Ciphertext-only at relay (§6.4); relay trust = zero
Relay logs metadata (who talks to whom) Envelope minimizes metadata; fallback to human channels if metadata exposure is unacceptable
Spam agents (Sybil) Consent-first inbound; key-based block; tier-based rate caps; §10 reputation
Replay Envelope seq/prev + request/grant/probe IDs; TTL bounds window
Key compromise Rotation doc signed by old key; revocation; grants are revocable
Key loss (no recovery path) §11 — open problem, mitigated by passkey custody
Government/relay takedown No single relay matters; card lists several; hint servers are swappable; human channels are last-resort always-on; nothing is required

9. Privacy

  • Owner identity is opt-in on public cards; private cards never leave the human channel.
  • Discovery topics derive from contact points: a listener learns only that someone who holds the address is asking — never identity, never content.
  • Directories see neither queries nor matches (OPRF); suggest friends is on by default and switchable off; list me is opt-in per directory.
  • Probes and invites are owner-initiated and rate-limited; received probes are archived with the consent record as an audit trail.
  • All content is E2E encrypted; relays see envelope metadata only.
  • Human-channel delivery renders a human-readable summary plus the machine envelope; the human can always read what their agent receives on their behalf.
  • Contact relationships are stored locally by each agent; no global social graph exists anywhere by construction.
  • Deletion: agents honor rdv/forget requests; relays hold ciphertext with TTLs and can garbage-collect without harm. Directory listings are revocable at will.

10. Anti-spam & reputation

  • Consent-first: unsolicited agents can't reach a human without a contact request the human approves. Default policy = prompt.
  • Key-based blocks are permanent and portable; re-registering under a new key resets reputation to zero (Sybil remains an open problem, §11).
  • Rate limits are grant-scoped (max_frequency); probes and invites carry their own caps (§5.2.1–5.2.2); violations revoke the grant automatically.
  • Optional reputation: a contact may attach verifiable vouches ("this key is also trusted by N of your existing contacts") — web-of-trust signals, never a global score.
  • Directory listings are optional and never required for discovery: hint topics and the probe work without any directory existing.

11. Open problems (honest)

  1. Key recovery. The key is the identity; losing it loses the agent's relationships. Passkey custody helps; a social-recovery design (m-of-n guardians) is deferred to v2.
  2. The fingerprint cliff. Humans won't compare hex strings. Consent UX must lean on channel context ("request came from the email you've exchanged 47 messages with") rather than manual verification.
  3. Sybil attacks. Key minting is free. Web-of-trust vouches mitigate; a clean general solution doesn't exist (email has the same disease).
  4. UI fragmentation. The consent tap must render on every platform — email deeplink, messaging button, agent app. This is product surface, not protocol, and it's the bulk of the real work.
  5. Absorption risk. A big lab can reimplement the idea. Accepted: the goal is interoperability, not ownership (MCP precedent).
  6. Cold start. A two-sided network needs both sides. Mitigations are designed in — machine-first discovery needs no permission to find, invites convert humans, suggestions surface existing friends — but nothing forces adoption. This is market risk, not protocol risk.
  7. Directory trust (small). Blinded lookups keep directories blind and they are swappable, but a listed agent still trusts its chosen directory to stay available and honest about exact matches. Running one is trivial; none is required.

12. Scope & phases

v0.1 (this document, rev 2): identity, cards, discovery (hint-server topics, mailbox probe, invite, optional directories), contact request/grant, envelope, Nostr primary, human-channel bootstrap.

v0.2: reference library (Rust or TypeScript), test vectors, interop tests.

v0.3: MCP server exposing the tool API (§13); dogfood on a live agent fleet — including discovery, probe and invite tools.

v0.4: fallback transports (ntfy/MQTT message transport), direct-channel negotiation, directory service + suggestions, rotation doc, revoke semantics.

v1.0: multi-vendor interop event ("two vendors' agents coordinate a dinner"), vouches, platform-profile discovery.

13. Reference implementation & distribution

Distribution model follows MCP: install one MCP server; your agent gains a calling card.

Proposed MCP tool API (v0.3):

Tool Purpose
rdv_init generate key + address card (or import existing)
rdv_publish_card publish/refresh card on chosen rendezvous
rdv_discover locate a peer from a contact point (hints → probe)
rdv_invite send a premade invite when no agent is found
rdv_suggestions list suggestion hits; flip the two opt-in switches
rdv_request_contact send a contact request (via hint or human channel)
rdv_pending_requests list inbound contact requests (for consent UX)
rdv_grant / rdv_revoke approve/reject/revoke contacts with tier+scope
rdv_send send an envelope (task, message, proposal)
rdv_inbox receive/ack envelopes (dedupe, ordering)
rdv_negotiate A2A task exchange lifecycle

A separate, optional directory service ships alongside for anyone who wants to run one (§4.5); it is not part of the required install.

14. Appendix

Why hint servers first? They are silent, machine-only, and instant: no email leaves a mailbox, no human is interrupted, and nothing lands in an inbox. Work is gated on the contact point the requester already holds, which is the same trust object as the rest of the protocol. Email is what people check; hint topics are what agents check. Until an inline protocol element is commonplace, the follow-up probe and invite cover the rest of the world.

Why Nostr? Key-first identity is identical to the agent-ID model; relays are dumb pipes by design; hundreds of free public relays exist; new event kinds are a supported extension point; no account system required.

Why not blockchain? A chain adds consensus, latency, cost, and a public-by-default ledger to a problem that needs pairwise secure messaging. Identity needs keys, not blocks; consent needs signatures, not a chain; discovery needs topics and hints, not a ledger. The one blockchain-flavored idea that survives — a tamper-evident consent audit log — is served by signed append-only documents, no consensus required.

Why not only email? Email is the universal fallback but is slow, metadata-leaky, and awkward for agents; Nostr gives low-latency relayed delivery with the same trust model. The card lists both; the network heals across them.

Why aren't directories a registry? A registry is an authority you must join. Directories are shards you may query: invented by anyone, stored blinded, swappable at will, skippable entirely. Discovery works with zero directories in existence.

Why does discovery not need consent? Finding is not contacting. A probe or topic query is answered per policy — often with silence — and no agent messages another without a signed grant. Consent governs communication, not lookups.

Naming. "Rendezvous" is a working title. The protocol's substance is vendor-neutral and unowned.
