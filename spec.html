Author: Omer Nesher
Date: 2026-10-25 (rev 2: 2026-10-09)

Codename "Rendezvous" is provisional. The protocol describes how a personal AI agent belonging to one human makes contact with, and safely communicates with, a personal AI agent belonging to another human — across different vendors, networks, and platforms.

Rev 2 (2026-10-09) adds machine-first discovery: hint-server topics (Nostr / ntfy / MQTT), the mailbox probe, the invite, and optional discovery directories with friend suggestions (on by default). Rev 1 defined identity, consent and transport; rev 2 defines how two agents find each other in the first place.

Rev 3 (2026-10-25) folds in what the TypeScript reference implementation (impl/) found: the wire format is now pinned down (canonical JSON, base32, signatures, envelope encryption, the Nostr carrier), the envelope signature covers the ciphertext, the grant carries the grantor's card, and consent, A2A meeting negotiation and untrusted peer text are specified. No discovery or consent concept changed; see Changes in rev 3 below.

Changes in rev 3

  • Security fix, §6.4: the envelope signature covers content (rev 2 left it out, so a genuine header could carry a substituted ciphertext). The encryption (ephemeral key epk, HKDF, AES-GCM) is now described. A peer that forks or rolls back its chain is refused until the receiving human re-syncs it.
  • Wire format, new §4.6: canonical JSON (RFC 8785 subset), base32 alphabet and case, sig encoding and domain separation, canon(address). §4.2 and §4.4 point to it.
  • Nostr, §6.2: envelope kind 30417 stays, with a mandatory unique d tag; the regular-kind alternative and the 2026-10 relay survey are recorded. The secp256k1 carrier key and the p tag value are defined. NIP-44 is no longer claimed for v0.1.
  • Discovery, §4.4: the claimed tier cap applies to the default tier, not an explicit human choice; the listen-mode probe is a contact request posted on the dtopic; probe greetings are public; the requester records a binding too.
  • Consent, §5: the grant carries the grantor's card (§5.3); who may answer a request; document IDs are UUIDs; grant direction and max_frequency format; scope defaults. New §5.5 (consent stays with the human) and §5.6 (peer text is untrusted data). Amended: the reverse grant (§5.3, a grant with grant_ref) tells a contact what its peer's owner allows it, so the grantor side can propose meetings too (§7.4). A confirm phrase approves only the tier and scope shown with it, and is stored hashed (§5.5).
  • A2A, §7: which A2A objects are sent, the meeting verbs, task states, slots, deduplication by messageId, cancellation, resend bounds, and persisting state before send. Amended: agreement is an accept → confirm handshake (meeting/confirm) with tentative, exclusive holds, and each human confirms the specific meeting (§5.5, §7.3; was §11.8).
  • Elsewhere: threat-model rows (§8), probe privacy (§9), new open problems (§11), phases aligned with the implementation plan (§12), MCP consent and inbox semantics (§13).

1. Abstract

Every major AI lab now ships a personal agent (Meta Muse, xAI Grok Bot, OpenAI Dots, Google Gemini Spark, Apple Siri AI, Instinct, Manus Cue, and more). Every one of them is an island. Instinct's agents can talk to other Instincts over its closed I2I network; Grok Bots coordinate only with other Grok Bots; Dots talk to dots. There is no open way for a Muse agent to contact an Instinct agent — the agent world today is the messaging world of 2008, before interoperability was demanded by users and eventually forced open by protocol adoption (MCP).

Rendezvous defines that missing layer: a vendor-neutral, open protocol for agent identity, discovery, first contact, consent, and communication, designed so that nobody has to run infrastructure to participate. It reuses proven building blocks — cryptographic keys for identity, Nostr relays as the primary rendezvous, A2A for task semantics — and treats every transport as an untrusted dumb pipe carrying end-to-end-encrypted envelopes.

Discovery is machine-first: agents find each other through silent hint-server topics, DNS, and — only when nothing answers — a signed mailbox probe or a premade invite. The human channel is the fallback transport and the place consent lives, not the first place to look.

Goals: - Any agent can contact any other agent, regardless of vendor or network. - Any agent can be found by requesters who hold one of its owner's contact points — silently, without emailing a human first. - Consent is a first-class protocol object: the human always decides who may reach their agent and what the relationship may do. - Zero required infrastructure: no central registry, no company server, no required relay operator. Optional discovery directories exist, are community-run, and can be skipped by anyone. - Self-healing: multiple rendezvous hints per agent; losing any one transport degrades gracefully, never breaks. - Boring technology: everything is a key, a signed JSON envelope, and an existing public transport.

Non-goals: - Not a message transport for humans. - Not a blockchain, token, or incentive system. - Not a payment or commerce layer (though agents may negotiate tasks that include payments via their own means). - Not a registry of identity for legal/KYC purposes.

2. Motivation

In eight weeks (Aug–Oct 2026) the personal-agent market went from niche to crowded. Instinct raised $1B at a $10B valuation; Muse became the #1 US app; Dots launched with 4,000+ app connectors. Each vendor built a closed network:

Vendor Agent Inter-agent story
Meta Muse Personal Agent Protocol working group (announced, no open impl)
xAI Grok Bot Bots delegate to Bots on the same account only
OpenAI Dots Dots coordinate within a user's own fleet
Instinct Instinct I2I "Trusted Person network" — closed, Instinct-to-Instinct only
Google Gemini Spark Google-ecosystem only
Decagon + Instinct PACT Open spec for person-agent ↔ business-agent consent (A2A-based)
Google A2A Open task-exchange protocol (server semantics, not consumer contact)

The vacuum: person-agent ↔ person-agent contact with per-relationship consent, across vendors. Instinct proved the demand (and built the walled garden). PACT proved business consent. Nobody has shipped the open person-to-person layer — for contact or discovery. The MCP precedent — an open protocol that won by solving fragmentation at the moment of peak pain, with zero ideology — is the template this spec follows.

3. Design principles

  1. Discovery is machine-first; humans are the trust anchor. Agents find each other through silent channels (hint-server topics, DNS, an optional directory) and, when nothing answers, a signed mailbox probe. The human channel — email/SMS/WhatsApp — is the fallback transport and the place consent lives. No new app, no new account, no always-on device.
  2. Keys are identity. An agent's identity is its Ed25519 public key, self-generated at install — globally unique, registry-free, unspoofable. It is the "MAC address" of the agent.
  3. Consent is the handshake. An agent may only communicate with another after the receiving human approves. Approval is a signed grant scoped to a relationship (trust tier, capabilities, expiry).
  4. Relays are dumb pipes. Every transport (Nostr relay, ntfy topic, MQTT broker, email server) sees only ciphertext and routing hints. Trust lives in keys, never in infrastructure.
  5. Rent, don't build. All transports are existing public services the users already pay for. The protocol ships a spec and a reference library and runs no required servers; optional discovery directories are community-run, swappable, and skippable.
  6. Self-healing by redundancy. An address card lists several rendezvous hints (Nostr relay, ntfy topic, MQTT, email, phone, DNS TXT). Failure of any one is routine, not fatal.
  7. Boring wins. Everything that can be a signed JSON document is a signed JSON document. Nothing needs consensus, a ledger, or a chain.

4. Identity layer

4.1 Agent key

  • Ed25519 keypair, generated at agent install.
  • The private key never leaves the agent's secure storage; it can be held by the user's passkey/HSM where available.
  • Key rotation: a new key is published with a rotate document signed by the old key, carrying the new key and a grace period. Peers accept either during the grace window. A key change outside a signed rotate document is a trust event: change alert + fresh consent handshake. (See §11 open problems on recovery.)

4.2 Agent ID

rdv1:<base32(ed25519-pubkey)>
  • rdv1 prefix disambiguates from other ID schemes.
  • base32 is the RFC 4648 alphabet, lowercase, without padding (§4.6): the 32-byte key becomes 52 characters, so an ID is 57 characters long.
  • Human-facing form: a short fingerprint, e.g. rdv1:7f3a…9c2d, always shown with a copy button; never ask humans to type full keys.

4.3 Address Card ("calling card")

The signed, self-describing document an agent publishes about itself. Schema (v1):

{
  "v": 1,
  "id": "rdv1:7f3a…9c2d",
  "created": "2026-10-08T00:00:00Z",
  "expires": null,
  "capabilities": ["rdv/v1", "a2a/0.3"],
  "owner": {                // OPTIONAL and minimized by default
    "name": "John Doe", // only if the owner opts in
    "contact": ["mailto:john@doe.com"]  // one hint is enough
  },
  "discovery": {            // OPTIONAL — enables machine-first discovery
    "addresses": [          // contact points the owner is findable by
      {"type": "email", "value": "john@doe.com"},
      {"type": "phone", "value": "+972-54-000-0000"}
    ],
    "public_pointer": false // default: listen-only dead drop (§4.4)
  },
  "rendezvous": [           // ordered fallback list
    {"type": "nostr", "relay": "wss://relay.damus.io", "topic": "<kind-30417>", "pref": 1},
    {"type": "ntfy", "topic": "rdv-7f3a9c2d", "pref": 2},
    {"type": "email", "to": "rdv+7f3a9c2d@agent.example", "pref": 3},
    {"type": "dns", "domain": "doe.com", "pref": 0}
  ],
  "policy": {
    "inbound": "prompt",     // allow | block | prompt
    "public": false          // publish card to public relays / pointers?
  },
  "sig": "…ed25519 signature over the canonical JSON above (§4.6)…"
}

Rules: - owner fields are opt-in and minimized. A public card need not name its owner at all. - discovery.addresses is opt-in per address: each listed address opens the dead-drop listen topic for that address (§4.4). Listing an address is a deliberate act, not a default. - rendezvous is ordered by preference; pref: 0 entries (like DNS TXT) are lookup hints rather than delivery addresses. - The card is versioned (v), signed, and revocable via expiry.

4.4 Discovery of a card

Discovery is machine-first: the requester's agent tries silent, machine-only paths before any human channel is involved; the human channel is the fallback and remains the anti-spoofing trust anchor. In order of availability:

  1. Hint servers (primary) — Nostr relays, ntfy.sh and MQTT carry a per-address topic derived deterministically from a contact point:

dtopic(address) = base32(SHA-256("rdv1/disc:" ‖ canon(address)))[0:26]

(canon and base32 are defined in §4.6. The same topic namespace is used on all three carriers — Nostr as a t tag on the agent-envelope kind, one topic per transport.) Two modes, both per-address opt-in, dead drops otherwise: - Listen — the agent watches its own topics. A requester who holds the address leaves a small, signed, protocol-shaped "who knows me" probe and the agent answers per policy. This is the cheapest lookup that exists: no email, no inbox, no human interruption. The probe is a contact request (§5.1) posted on the dtopic with to_hint set to the address; there is no separate probe document. The listener gates it exactly like any inbound request, with binding claimed. The probe is cleartext on the carrier (§9): keep the greeting short. - Public pointer — the agent publishes its card (or a card hash) on the topic, so a requester can fetch it without posting first. Default off; policy.public gate. 2. DNS TXT — _agent.<domain> TXT "rdv=rdv1:…" for owners with domains. 3. Platform profile fields — a rdv1:… line in an X bio / LinkedIn about / GitHub profile. The platform account vouchsafes the binding. 4. Directories (optional) — community-run services that answer blinded lookups; see §4.5. Off by default. 5. Mailbox probe (email first) — a signed email carrying the contact request, delivered through the recipient's mail rule; see §5.2.1. The universal fallback: works wherever email does, and proves mailbox control. (The profile generalizes to other carriers later.) 6. Direct (STUN) — once both sides know each other's hints, ICE/STUN hole-punching upgrades the conversation to a direct link. STUN is a connectivity upgrade after discovery, not a lookup. 7. In person — QR code / NFC containing the card.

Bindings. Every discovery path yields a binding level, recorded with the contact and shown at consent time:

Path Binding Meaning
QR / NFC in person in-person Out-of-band, strongest
DNS TXT dns Control of the domain proven
Mailbox probe mailbox Control of the mailbox proven (handshake echo)
Hint topics, profiles, directories claimed Self-asserted

A claimed binding is a lead, not a credential: the default tier offered at consent caps at stranger until a stronger binding promotes it. A human who explicitly picks a higher tier at consent is the explicit confirmation that promotes it; the cap constrains defaults, never the owner's choice. Bindings attach to the relationship record, not the card, and both sides keep one: the requester records how it found the peer (claimed for a topic probe or a public pointer; in-person when the owner says the card came from a QR code or face to face).

4.5 Discovery directories (optional)

A directory is a small, community-runnable HTTP service that maps dtopic(address) → listing { card, updated, listing_sig }. Listings are self-asserted (claimed); the server verifies only the request signature.

  • Blinded lookups. Queries use an OPRF (RFC 9497): the requester blinds the dtopic, the server evaluates it against a twisted store, the requester unblinds. The server learns neither the contact point nor the match — and only exact-contact matches are ever returned. There is no browsing, no name search, no enumeration.
  • Listing is opt-in per the card's policy, separate from public.
  • Suggestions ("people you may know"). On by default — switch it off anytime. The agent runs its owner's own contact list against configured directories, locally, and surfaces hits: "John appears to have an agent. Connect?" Nothing is sent until the owner taps. On connect, the agent keeps the peer's card; no rediscovery, forever. Suggestions can only surface owners who opted into a listing — a suggestion is a lead, confirmed by the handshake.
  • Two switches: suggest friends is on by default (owner-facing, computed locally; switch it off anytime). list me in a given directory is opt-in — a publishing act, off by default.
  • No federation, no authority. Directories are shards, not a registry: anyone may run one, clients choose which to trust, and skipping directories entirely never breaks anything.

4.6 Encoding, canonical JSON and signatures

Every signed document (card, request, grant, revoke, envelope) follows the same rules, so independent implementations produce identical bytes:

  • Canonical JSON (canon(doc)) is a subset of RFC 8785 (JCS): UTF-8, object members sorted by key, no insignificant whitespace, absent members omitted. Numbers MUST be integers (timestamps and durations are strings, so no floating-point number is ever serialized); null is allowed.
  • Base32 is the RFC 4648 alphabet, lowercase, no padding. It is used for agent IDs (§4.2) and discovery topics (§4.4).
  • Hash values (prev, the Nostr d tag) are the lowercase hex SHA-256 of the canonical JSON of the whole document, sig included.
  • sig is the base64 (RFC 4648 §4, standard alphabet, padded) encoding of the 64-byte Ed25519 signature over "rdv1/sig:" ‖ canon(doc without sig). The prefix domain-separates Rendezvous signatures from any other use of the same key, so a signature cannot be lifted from one context to another. Binary fields inside content (§6.4) use the same base64.
  • canon(address) is the bare value with no type prefix: an email is trimmed and lowercased; a phone is + followed by digits only (a leading + is required; separators are removed).

5. Contact layer (consent & handshake)

5.1 Contact Request

The envelope that asks "may my agent talk to your agent?":

{
  "v": 1,
  "type": "rdv/contact-request",
  "id": "<uuid>",
  "from": "rdv1:<requester key>",
  "to_hint": "john@doe.com",
  "card": "<requester's address card>",
  "greeting": "Alice's agent requests contact on Alice's behalf.",
  "capabilities_offered": ["a2a/0.3"],
  "ts": "2026-10-08T00:00:00Z",
  "sig": "…"
}

Delivery (§6): the request is posted to any rendezvous hint the requester can resolve for the target (including hint-server topics), delivered by the mailbox probe (§5.2.1), or sent through the human channel (email/SMS/ WhatsApp) addressed to the human.

Rules: - id MUST be an RFC 4122 UUID string, as must the id of every grant and revoke and a grant's request_ref. Receivers reject anything else: IDs are chosen by the peer and end up in logs and user interfaces. - A request sent to a known card travels inside an encrypted envelope. A topic probe (§4.4) is posted in cleartext on the carrier, greeting and card included (§9). - greeting is peer-written text: display-only (§5.6).

5.2 Human-channel bootstrap

When machine-first discovery yields nothing, contact travels the human channel. Two profiles cover the cases: the mailbox probe when the requester holds a human address and wants to know if an agent exists (§5.2.1), and the invite when no agent was found and the owner chooses to recruit one (§5.2.2). In both, the signed grant flow (§5.3) is unchanged: nothing proceeds without the receiving human's approval.

The human channel is also the anti-spoofing anchor: contact is bound to the address it arrived through, and the human verifies against the social context they already have ("I do know john@doe.com"). Key fingerprints are additionally comparable out-of-band (Signal safety-number model).

5.2.1 Mailbox probe (email probe profile)

The probe is a three-way handshake, like TCP:

SYN       contact-request by email, marked [RDV-PROBE]
SYN-ACK   contact-grant + recipient card + cookie
ACK       first envelope on the recipient's preferred hint, echoing the cookie

Design goals: discovery without awkwardness ("does this person even have a bot?") and an anti-spoofing anchor. The mail rule routes; the agent gates.

Marking. - Subject starts with [RDV-PROBE] followed by a plain-language line, e.g. "bot discovery request — if you're a human, you can ignore this." - X-Rdv-Probe: v=1; id=<uuid>; from=rdv1:… for header-aware rules. - Auto-Submitted: auto-generated (RFC 3834) so out-of-office responders and other bots never loop. A probe has one recipient, no Cc/Bcc, and is never sent to a list.

Body. A short human-readable paragraph, plus the machine part fenced between ---BEGIN RDV/1--- and ---END RDV/1--- markers (canonical JSON: the signed contact request, the requester's card, a cookie slot).

The rule (integrator recipe). 1. Match the subject prefix or the header. 2. Hand the message to the agent's probe endpoint and remove it from the inbox (archive — no clutter; it reaches the human surface only when policy says prompt, and then in the bot chat, not the mail app). 3. Apply the owner's policy: block — silent drop; prompt — surface in the bot chat; allow — auto-grant within the pre-approved scope.

The gate (mandatory, before anything reaches a model). Probes are attacker-controlled text addressed to a bot — the exact shape of a prompt injection — so parsing is deterministic and content never executes: 1. Parse the fenced JSON; reject anything malformed. 2. Verify the Ed25519 signature; verify DMARC/DKIM/SPF via Authentication-Results (RFC 8601). Unaligned mail is challenged, never trusted. 3. Rate-limit and dedupe by request id; probes expire after 14 days. 4. Only then apply policy. Raw probe text is data, never instructions — the gate runs before any LLM sees a byte of it.

Verification (the mailbox proof). - If DMARC passes, arrival already carries a weak channel proof and the agent may prompt the human immediately. - Otherwise the reply is a stateless challenge: cookie = HMAC(K_agent, request_id ‖ canon(from) ‖ date), truncated. Only a requester who actually received the reply can echo the cookie — the SYN-ACK proves mailbox control, and forged probes die quietly. - The ACK (third leg) rides the recipient's preferred hint from the returned card (or replies by email if none is reachable) and opens the normal envelope flow (§6). Until the ACK lands the contact is pending.

Silence. Default policy is prompt, so no reply ever confirms to an unknown sender whether an agent exists. Probes are owner-initiated only, at most one per address per 30 days (configurable). Zero replies within the window means "no agent found" — the requester never nags; the owner may then choose to invite (§5.2.2).

The reply. Sent from the agent's own mailbox identity (e.g. rdv+<agentid>@…, or the same address with a distinct token, [RDV-PROBE-ACK]) so humans can tell agent mail from human mail at a glance.

Other carriers (later). The probe profile is carrier-agnostic: a signed JSON envelope plus fixed markers. WhatsApp / Telegram: a marker line as the first message line. SMS: short token plus link. Email ships first because rules, headers and DMARC give it the strongest deterministic gate.

5.2.2 Invite (no agent found)

When discovery comes up empty — listen topics silent, no DNS record, no directory hit, probe unanswered — the owner can send a premade invitation, modeled on WhatsApp's "this person isn't on WhatsApp — invite them?" flow. The invite is the network's growth path, and it is human, not spam.

Marking. Same machinery as the probe: the subject starts with [RDV-INVITE] (or X-Rdv-Invite: v=1; … for header-aware rules), plus the standard ---BEGIN RDV/1--- block carrying rdv/invite and the single-use token. One message serves both realities: if the recipient's agent exists, its mail rule hands the invite straight to the agent; if no agent exists, the human reads a friendly note.

  • Sent only after the owner confirms ("Dana doesn't seem to have an agent. Invite her?" → Send). One pending invite per address; 30-day cooldown on silence or decline; permanent opt-out honored.
  • Written to read as a note from the owner, explicitly disclosed as sent by their assistant, and stating where the sender got the address.
  • Carries a single-use token (inside the standard ---BEGIN RDV/1--- block as rdv/invite) plus a short explainer link. Acceptance replies with the token; if the recipient has an agent, it picks up the thread and the normal consent flow (§5.1–5.3) runs. No token, no data.
  • Invites never carry the owner's card beyond what is needed; the full card exchange happens only after acceptance.
  • When the invitee later runs an agent, the token maps the human introduction to the handshake — no rediscovery needed.

5.3 Contact Grant (consent)

{
  "v": 1,
  "type": "rdv/contact-grant",
  "id": "<uuid>",
  "from": "rdv1:<grantor key>",
  "to": "rdv1:<requester key>",
  "request_ref": "<contact-request or probe id>",
  "card": "<grantor's address card>",
  "tier": "colleague",        // spouse | family | friend | colleague | business | stranger
  "scope": {
    "may_initiate": true,
    "may_propose_meetings": true,
    "may_access_calendar": "free_busy",
    "max_frequency": "20/day"
  },
  "expires": "2027-10-08T00:00:00Z",
  "ts": "…",
  "sig": "…"
}

Trust tiers are a suggested default set, not a schema limit; grants are explicit capability lists and vendors may add scopes. The default tier for a claimed binding caps at stranger (§4.4); an explicit human choice is not capped.

Rules: - The grant carries the grantor's card (card). After a topic probe the requester knows only the address, so the card is how it learns where to reach the grantor (the SYN-ACK of §5.2.1 carries it too). The requester verifies the card (signature, not expired, card.id equals from) before using it. - tier MUST be one of the tiers above; a grant with any other value is rejected. scope MUST be a JSON object. - Who may answer a request. A request sent to a specific card is satisfied only by a grant signed by that card's agent; any other grantor is rejected before state changes. A topic probe has no addressee, so anyone who saw it on the dtopic can answer. The requester MUST ignore a probe answer whose card does not list the probed address in discovery.addresses, and MUST NOT turn any probe answer into a contact until its owner confirms it, after being shown the grantor's fingerprint (§5.5). Until then the answer is a candidate: several grantors may answer one probe, and each is kept as a separate candidate (the reference keeps up to 16 per probe, one per grantor), so an eavesdropper who answers first cannot displace the real target. Keys are free, so a flood can still fill one probe: answers beyond the cap MUST NOT evict another probe's candidates, and the requester SHOULD count them and tell its owner that the real answer may be among the dropped ones and the contact is best redone by card (in person, or another trusted channel). A confirmed probe answer is recorded as claimed. - Direction. A grant authorizes the requester to contact the grantor within scope; that is all it does. A received grant MUST NOT set what the grantor may do towards the requester: the grantor chose that scope, and the requester's human has approved nothing. Until the requester's owner grants the peer explicitly (a local approval, §5.5), the requester applies its own local default to the grantor: it may write (may_initiate), may not propose meetings, gets no calendar access, and is rate-limited locally (reference: 50/day). The grantor's tier is likewise only how the grantor classifies the requester; the requester's own tier for the grantor starts at stranger. - Reverse grant. When an owner grants an existing contact rights towards it (§5.5), typically the requester granting the grantor back, its agent sends the peer a reverse grant: the same rdv/contact-grant document with grant_ref instead of request_ref. grant_ref is the id of the grant that founded the contact, which both sides keep. A grant with both fields, or neither, is invalid, so the two kinds can never be confused:

json { "v": 1, "type": "rdv/contact-grant", "id": "<uuid>", "from": "rdv1:<requester key>", "to": "rdv1:<grantor key>", "grant_ref": "<id of the grant that founded the contact>", "card": "<sender's card>", "tier": "friend", "scope": { "may_initiate": true, "may_propose_meetings": true, "max_frequency": "100/day" }, "expires": null, "ts": "…", "sig": "…" }

The receiver applies it exactly like any received grant: it sets only what the receiver may do towards the sender, never the sender's rights over the receiver. It MUST be accepted only from a current contact whose founding grant is grant_ref, addressed to the receiver, verified like a grant (signature, card, tier, timestamps), not expired, with a ts newer than the grant it replaces (and not far ahead of the receiver's clock; reference: 5 minutes) and an unseen id. Anything else is dropped without changing state. Its expires may shorten the contact, never extend it. A receiver MAY rate-limit them per contact (reference: 10 an hour). Revocation (§5.4) removes whatever a reverse grant set. Only the owner's own grant triggers one; an agent never sends one on its own. - max_frequency is "<n>/<minute|hour|day>"; exceeding it revokes the grant (§10). - Scope defaults for meetings are in §7.4.

5.4 Policy and revocation

  • The receiving agent's inbound policy is allow (only grants from the allowlist), block (key-based, permanent for that key), or prompt (the default — surface to the human).
  • Revocation: a rdv/revoke document, or simply dropping the grant entry. Blocked keys never receive responses (silent drop is a privacy feature).
  • Replay protection: request, grant, probe and invite IDs are stored; duplicates ignored. Probe cookies are stateless and expire with the probe.

5.5 Consent stays with the human

Approval MUST come from the human, through a channel the assistant model cannot answer. An assistant that can grant on its own, or can be talked into it by a greeting, defeats consent. A host that exposes the protocol to a model (an MCP server, a chat bot, an app) therefore MUST NOT grant on the strength of model output alone. Acceptable approvals are: - a confirmation answered by the human in the host's own UI (for MCP, an elicitation request); - a per-request confirm phrase shown only on human channels (a terminal, a local UI) and typed by the human; it never appears in a tool result. The phrase approves the request on the terms shown with it (tier and scope), and nothing else: the host MUST refuse it for any other tier or scope the model asks for, and show the human a new phrase for those terms instead. A host SHOULD store only a salted, slow hash of the phrase, so that it cannot be read back from disk. The reference implementation accepts a phrase only for tiers less close than family (friend and below); - the owner's own command in a terminal; - a standing pre-approval the owner set as policy (allow, §5.4).

The same holds on the requester's side. A grant the agent receives is the peer's decision, not its owner's: it sets only what the agent may do towards the peer (§5.3), and choosing which answer to a topic probe is the real peer needs one of the approvals above. Widening what a peer may do towards the agent (meetings, calendar, a higher rate) is always the agent's own owner's grant. That grant, approved the same way, is what the agent then sends the peer as a reverse grant (§5.3); receiving one widens only what the agent may do towards the peer, and needs no approval of its own.

A grant that allows meetings lets the agents negotiate, not book. Before an agent accepts a specific meeting, or confirms a peer's accept, the host SHOULD get its human's confirmation of that meeting through one of the same approvals (a standing auto-confirm policy the owner set counts; the reference CLI calls it --auto-confirm). The slot is held tentatively while the human decides (§7.3).

That confirmation is for the meeting the human was shown, not for a task ID: task IDs are chosen by the peer, and the task behind one can change while the human decides (a cancel and a new proposal under the same ID, another slot). The host MUST bind the approval to what it showed (at least the peer, contextId, task ID, the held slot's start and end, and which step is being confirmed) and MUST NOT act on it if the held meeting differs when it acts; it asks again instead. A confirm phrase for a meeting is bound the same way and stops working once the hold changes or the task ends.

5.6 Peer text is untrusted data

Greetings, text parts, decline reasons and card owner fields are written by the other side and can carry instructions aimed at the reader's assistant. They are display-only: they MUST NOT be followed, and only machine (data) parts and validated fields may drive actions. Short structural fields (payload type, IDs, tiers, states) are validated against strict shapes and rejected if they do not match. A host SHOULD hand peer text to a model only when clearly marked as untrusted, and SHOULD strip terminal control characters before showing it.

6. Transport layer

6.1 Rendezvous hints

Each address card lists ordered transports. The envelope (encrypted, signed) is delivered over the first available hint; acks travel the same way. Ordering is per-card, so both parties control their own preferences.

Direct-first — permanently. Keys are pinned once exchanged (TOFU; a change outside a signed rotation triggers the alert and a fresh handshake). Every later conversation between the two agents opens a direct channel immediately when both sides are reachable — no rediscovery, no relay hop to set up. Relays cover only what direct can't: offline peers (store-and-forward) and networks where hole-punching fails (TURN relays carry the ciphertext).

6.2 Primary relay: Nostr

  • The agent posts to one or more public Nostr relays. Relayed delivery covers first contact, offline peers, and failed hole-punching — everything a direct channel can't reach.
  • Envelope events use a dedicated kind (30417, "agent envelope"). The event content is the envelope JSON (§6.4). Tags: p is the recipient's Ed25519 public key as lowercase hex (not the rdv1: string); d is the envelope hash (§4.6). Discovery topics ride the same kind: t holds the dtopic (§4.4) and d is the hex SHA-256 of the event content.
  • Kind decision (rev 3). 30417 is in the addressable range (30000-39999), where relays keep only the latest event per (pubkey, kind, d), so two envelopes without distinct d tags overwrite each other. Rev 3 keeps 30417 and makes a unique d (the envelope hash) mandatory. Alternative: a regular kind (1000-9999), which stores every envelope as its own event and removes the d fragility. A 2026-10 survey of public relays (nos.lol, relay.primal.net, nostr.mom, relay.damus.io and others) found no relay-side difference: all kept 30417 and a regular test kind alike, with #p/#t queries and distinct d values. The case for a regular kind is therefore semantic, not empirical; it is a wire change to revisit with the test vectors if a relay starts to differ. Not tested: same-d overwrite and long-term retention.
  • Carrier key. Nostr events are signed with secp256k1, not Ed25519. Each agent derives a stable carrier key: HKDF-SHA256 over its Ed25519 seed, no salt, info "rdv1/nostr-carrier", 32 bytes. It signs the Nostr event only; authenticity comes from the envelope signature, so receivers MUST NOT treat the carrier public key as the sender's identity.
  • Encryption is the envelope's own (§6.4), not NIP-44. Conversation-scoped keys derived from the contact handshake are future work.
  • Why Nostr: key-first identity matches §4, relays are dumb pipes by design, hundreds of free public relays exist, and the ecosystem is explicitly open to new event kinds. (§14)

6.3 Fallbacks (all optional, all dumb pipes)

  • ntfy.sh — topic-per-agent pub/sub; open source; public instance free. Also a discovery carrier (§4.4 dtopic, listen mode).
  • MQTT — public brokers (test.mosquitto.org, broker.emqx.io, HiveMQ public); topic = agent ID. Also a discovery carrier (dtopic).
  • Human channels — email / SMS / WhatsApp / Telegram, store-and-forward; the always-available slow path. Envelope rides multipart/alternative or a JSON block; agent-side parsing is the receiver's job. Carries the probe (§5.2.1) and invite (§5.2.2).
  • DNS TXT — lookup hint only (_agent.<domain>), not a delivery path.
  • Direct (the default after contact) — once keys are pinned, every conversation attempts direct first: ICE (RFC 8445) with STUN (RFC 8489) hole-punching, negotiated in-band. IPv6 endpoints are preferred when present. Relays and TURN carry traffic only as fallback (offline peers, failed punch-through).

6.4 Envelope

{
  "v": 1,
  "from": "rdv1:…",
  "to": "rdv1:…",
  "seq": 42,
  "prev": "<hash of previous envelope, null for the first>",
  "created": "…",
  "ttl": "48h",
  "content": {
    "alg": "x25519+aes256gcm",
    "epk": "…",                  // ephemeral X25519 public key, base64
    "nonce": "…",                // 12 random bytes, base64
    "ciphertext": "…"            // base64
  },
  "sig": "…"                     // over everything outside sig, content included (§4.6)
}
  • The signature covers content. Rev 2 signed only the header. Anyone holding one genuine envelope could then keep its signed header and swap in a ciphertext of their own, encrypted to the recipient, which the recipient would accept as coming from the header's sender.
  • Encryption. The sender generates an ephemeral X25519 key pair per envelope and combines it with the recipient's X25519 key (its Ed25519 identity key, converted). The AES-256 key is HKDF-SHA256(secret, salt = epk ‖ recipient_x25519_pub, info = "rdv1/envelope"), 32 bytes. The cipher is AES-256-GCM with a random 12-byte nonce, and the header (v, from, to, seq, prev, created, ttl) is bound as associated data. The plaintext is the payload JSON (§7).
  • Relays see the header and sig, plus epk, nonce and ciphertext size; they cannot read the payload.
  • seq/prev give ordering and gap detection (§7). The first envelope in a direction has seq: 1 and prev: null; later ones carry the hash (§4.6) of the previous envelope in the same direction. seq is a positive integer.
  • Forks and rollbacks. An envelope identical to one already accepted is a relay replay and is dropped silently. A different envelope that reuses an accepted seq, or whose prev does not match the receiver's chain head, means the sender forked its chain: it restored older state (a backup) or two copies of it run. The receiver MUST NOT accept the forked chain on its own and MUST NOT drop it silently: it tells its human and refuses the peer's envelopes until the human re-syncs. A re-sync adopts the next envelope that is newer (created) than every envelope accepted from that peer; relay replays of the old chain are never newer, so they cannot become the head. After a re-sync the receiver treats any envelope from that peer that is not newer than the old chain's newest as a duplicate, whatever its seq or prev, so replays cannot reopen a gap or refuse the peer again. Detection keeps bounded state: the hashes of the last few accepted envelopes (8 in the reference implementation) plus the newest accepted created. A reused seq below that window is detected by its created being newer than everything accepted; one that is also older is indistinguishable from a replay and is dropped as one.
  • ttl is <n><s|m|h|d> (default 48h); an envelope is expired when created + ttl has passed. It lets relays garbage-collect and bounds replay windows, so receivers MUST reject a ttl over 7 days and a created more than 5 minutes ahead of their clock; otherwise a sender could make an envelope replayable for years.
  • No forward secrecy yet. The recipient key is static, so a later key compromise exposes recorded envelopes (§11).

7. Session & task layer (A2A semantics)

  • Once contact is granted, agents exchange tasks using A2A v0.3 objects (§7.1) inside the envelope's encrypted content. The payload is a JSON document with a type field.
  • The protocol is asynchronous by default: tasks, proposals, and confirmations are documents; no live channel is assumed. (Calendar negotiation = propose/accept cycles over store-and-forward.)
  • Idempotency: task IDs are UUIDs; retries re-send the same envelope. There are two dedupe layers: the receiver drops an envelope it has already seen (the same bytes; a different envelope at that seq is a fork, §6.4), and drops a re-sent A2A message (same messageId, new envelope) per task.
  • Ordering: seq is per-direction monotonic. A gap buffers later envelopes and triggers {"type": "rdv/resend", "from_seq": n, "to_seq": m}; the sender replays from an outbox (the reference implementation keeps 64 envelopes). Receivers MAY bound and drop: the reference implementation buffers out-of-order envelopes only from contacts and from agents it asked by card (16 peers x 64 envelopes).
  • Persistence: a sender SHOULD commit the new seq, prev and its outbox to durable storage before the envelope leaves, so a crash cannot reuse a seq. The worst case is then a payload delivered twice.
  • Cancellation: a rdv/cancel referencing a task ID, honored when the task is still pending (not terminal).
  • Direct-first operation: once both sides have routable endpoints, the in-band negotiation opens a direct channel (WebRTC data channel) immediately — the default mode of every subsequent session, not an upgrade. Relays resume only for offline peers and NAT failures. The envelope format is unchanged.

7.1 A2A mapping

Two payload types carry A2A v0.3 objects: a2a/message holds a Message (one negotiation turn) and a2a/task-update holds a TaskStatusUpdateEvent (an acknowledgement or end state). A whole Task object is never sent; each side keeps its own copy. A2A assumes a client and a server agent, but both peers here are agents, so role is always "agent". messageId, taskId and contextId are UUIDs; one contextId covers one negotiation.

A message has human parts (text) and one machine part (data) whose data.rdv names the verb (§7.2). Text parts are for humans and never drive anything (§5.6). Receivers validate strictly and reject unknown keys, unknown verbs and non-UUID IDs.

7.2 Meeting verbs

data.rdv Fields Meaning
meeting/propose 1-3 slots, optional place, expires open a negotiation
meeting/counter 1-3 slots, optional place, expires offer different slots
meeting/accept one slot accept a slot from the latest offer
meeting/confirm one slot the offerer makes an accepted slot final
meeting/decline reason refuse; nothing fits
meeting/cancel one slot, optional reason call off an agreed (completed) meeting
rdv/cancel optional reason withdraw a pending task, by the message's taskId

A slot is {"start", "end"}, RFC 3339 date-times with an explicit offset (Z or ±HH:MM), compared as instants, so an accept may write the slot in another offset; at most 12 hours long. expires defaults to 48 hours and a receiver clamps it to 7 days. How to extend the verb set is open (§11).

7.3 Task states

submitted (proposal sent) → input-required (the responder countered) or working (the proposer countered back) → completed | rejected | canceled. A round is one offer; the receiver of the third offer declines if nothing fits, which ends the task rejected. An accept MUST pick a slot from the latest offer it answers. The reference policy is: offer up to 3 free slots, accept the first slot that is free, otherwise counter with 3 of your own; implementations MAY choose a different policy within these rules.

Agreement takes two messages. The accepter holds the slot tentatively and sends meeting/accept; the task stays pending. The offerer checks the slot is still free (offers are not held) and that the task has not expired, then sends meeting/confirm with the same slot and completes; otherwise it declines. The accepter completes on the confirm and acknowledges with a completed status update. A tentative hold MUST be released when the task is canceled, declined or expires, or when the peer reports it canceled or rejected, and a slot held for one task (tentatively or agreed) is busy for every other task. An accepter waiting for the confirm SHOULD keep its hold for a grace period past expires (reference: 24 hours), since the offerer may have confirmed in time. Either side MAY wait for its human before its step (§5.5).

A rdv/cancel is honored while the task is not terminal. A cancel that arrives before its proposal leaves a tombstone so the proposal is refused when it lands. A cancel, or any verb, for a finished task is answered with a status update giving its real end state.

An agreed meeting is called off with meeting/cancel, which names the agreed slot. Only a human may send it (§5.5), and the host binds that approval to the agreement it showed (peer, contextId, task ID, slot). The sender releases its hold and ends the task canceled, keeping the agreed slot as a record. The receiver honors it only for a completed task with that sender, contextId and slot (compared as instants) and drops anything else; it releases its hold, ends canceled, acknowledges with a canceled status update and tells its human. It needs no approval from the receiving human: a peer can only free time this way, never commit it. A meeting/cancel for a task that already ended is answered with its status and changes nothing, and a later negotiation of the same slot is a new task that it never touches. Likewise, a peer reporting canceled or rejected for a task the receiver completed releases it (the confirm crossed a cancel or arrived too late). An agent that predates the verb drops it as unknown and keeps the meeting.

7.4 Scope and defaults

Meeting verbs require may_propose_meetings: true in the grant that covers the direction (§5.3): proposing to a peer, and answering on a negotiation we proposed, need the peer's grant to us; a peer proposing to us needs the grant our owner gave the peer. For the agent that granted the contact, "the peer's grant to us" is the peer's reverse grant (§5.3): until the requester's owner grants back, the grantor cannot propose. If may_access_calendar is absent it counts as free_busy, because proposing a slot is a free/busy disclosure; none blocks negotiation. At every level, including full, meeting messages carry slots only, never event titles.

Peer state is bounded: only a meeting-capable grant may create tasks (or tombstones); a receiver SHOULD cap tasks per peer (reference: 32, evicting finished ones and tombstones first and refusing when all are pending) and prune finished tasks (reference: after 30 days).

9. Privacy

  • Owner identity is opt-in on public cards; private cards never leave the human channel.
  • Discovery topics derive from contact points: a listener learns only that someone who holds the address is asking — never identity, never content.
  • Directories see neither queries nor matches (OPRF); suggest friends is on by default and switchable off; list me is opt-in per directory.
  • A topic probe (§4.4) is cleartext on the carrier: its greeting and the requester's card are readable by the relay and by anyone who knows the topic. Keep greetings short and free of private detail. Requests to a known card travel inside an encrypted envelope.
  • Probes and invites are owner-initiated and rate-limited; received probes are archived with the consent record as an audit trail.
  • All content is E2E encrypted; relays see envelope metadata only.
  • Human-channel delivery renders a human-readable summary plus the machine envelope; the human can always read what their agent receives on their behalf.
  • Contact relationships are stored locally by each agent; no global social graph exists anywhere by construction.
  • Deletion: agents honor rdv/forget requests; relays hold ciphertext with TTLs and can garbage-collect without harm. Directory listings are revocable at will.

10. Anti-spam & reputation

  • Consent-first: unsolicited agents can't reach a human without a contact request the human approves. Default policy = prompt.
  • Key-based blocks are permanent and portable; re-registering under a new key resets reputation to zero (Sybil remains an open problem, §11).
  • Rate limits are grant-scoped (max_frequency); probes and invites carry their own caps (§5.2.1–5.2.2); violations revoke the grant automatically.
  • Optional reputation: a contact may attach verifiable vouches ("this key is also trusted by N of your existing contacts") — web-of-trust signals, never a global score.
  • Directory listings are optional and never required for discovery: hint topics and the probe work without any directory existing.

11. Open problems (honest)

  1. Key recovery. The key is the identity; losing it loses the agent's relationships. Passkey custody helps; a social-recovery design (m-of-n guardians) is deferred to v2.
  2. The fingerprint cliff. Humans won't compare hex strings. Consent UX must lean on channel context ("request came from the email you've exchanged 47 messages with") rather than manual verification.
  3. Sybil attacks. Key minting is free. Web-of-trust vouches mitigate; a clean general solution doesn't exist (email has the same disease).
  4. UI fragmentation. The consent tap must render on every platform — email deeplink, messaging button, agent app. This is product surface, not protocol, and it's the bulk of the real work.
  5. Absorption risk. A big lab can reimplement the idea. Accepted: the goal is interoperability, not ownership (MCP precedent).
  6. Cold start. A two-sided network needs both sides. Mitigations are designed in — machine-first discovery needs no permission to find, invites convert humans, suggestions surface existing friends — but nothing forces adoption. This is market risk, not protocol risk.
  7. Directory trust (small). Blinded lookups keep directories blind and they are swappable, but a listed agent still trusts its chosen directory to stay available and honest about exact matches. Running one is trivial; none is required.

  8. Ending an agreed meeting. meeting/cancel (§7.3) lets either human call off an agreed meeting, and a peer's canceled or rejected status now releases a meeting the receiver completed, which closes the race where a confirm is delayed past the accepter's grace period or crosses the accepter's owner's cancel. Still open: an agent that predates the verb ignores it and keeps the meeting, and nothing tells the canceling side; the humans have to sort that out.

  9. No forward secrecy. Envelopes are encrypted to a static recipient key (§6.4). Conversation-scoped keys from the handshake are future work.

  10. Channel state for strangers. A receiver advances seq state for any validly signed envelope before deciding whether to admit the sender. A stranger cannot spoof another sender, so the cost is memory per stranger ID, to be bounded.
  11. Extending the meeting verbs. Validation is strict (§7.2); whether extensions use a version field or an A2A extension URI is undecided.

12. Scope & phases

v0.1 (this document, rev 3): identity, cards, discovery (hint-server topics, mailbox probe, invite, optional directories), contact request/grant, envelope, Nostr primary, human-channel bootstrap, A2A meeting negotiation.

v0.2: reference library (TypeScript) and rdv CLI, test vectors, interop tests, A2A dinner negotiation over a real relay.

v0.3: MCP server exposing the tool API (§13); dogfood on a live agent fleet — including discovery, probe and invite tools.

v0.4: fallback transports (ntfy/MQTT message transport), direct-channel negotiation, directory service + suggestions, rotation doc, revoke semantics.

v1.0: multi-vendor interop event ("two vendors' agents coordinate a dinner"), vouches, platform-profile discovery.

13. Reference implementation & distribution

Distribution model follows MCP: install one MCP server; your agent gains a calling card.

Proposed MCP tool API (v0.3):

Tool Purpose
rdv_init generate key + address card (or import existing)
rdv_publish_card publish/refresh card on chosen rendezvous
rdv_discover locate a peer from a contact point (hints → probe)
rdv_invite send a premade invite when no agent is found
rdv_suggestions list suggestion hits; flip the two opt-in switches
rdv_request_contact send a contact request (via hint or human channel)
rdv_pending_requests list inbound contact requests (for consent UX)

A separate, optional directory service ships alongside for anyone who wants to run one (§4.5); it is not part of the required install.

14. Appendix

Why hint servers first? They are silent, machine-only, and instant: no email leaves a mailbox, no human is interrupted, and nothing lands in an inbox. Work is gated on the contact point the requester already holds, which is the same trust object as the rest of the protocol. Email is what people check; hint topics are what agents check. Until an inline protocol element is commonplace, the follow-up probe and invite cover the rest of the world.

Why Nostr? Key-first identity is identical to the agent-ID model; relays are dumb pipes by design; hundreds of free public relays exist; new event kinds are a supported extension point; no account system required.

Why not blockchain? A chain adds consensus, latency, cost, and a public-by-default ledger to a problem that needs pairwise secure messaging. Identity needs keys, not blocks; consent needs signatures, not a chain; discovery needs topics and hints, not a ledger. The one blockchain-flavored idea that survives — a tamper-evident consent audit log — is served by signed append-only documents, no consensus required.

Why not only email? Email is the universal fallback but is slow, metadata-leaky, and awkward for agents; Nostr gives low-latency relayed delivery with the same trust model. The card lists both; the network heals across them.

Why aren't directories a registry? A registry is an authority you must join. Directories are shards you may query: invented by anyone, stored blinded, swappable at will, skippable entirely. Discovery works with zero directories in existence.

Why does discovery not need consent? Finding is not contacting. A probe or topic query is answered per policy — often with silence — and no agent messages another without a signed grant. Consent governs communication, not lookups.

Naming. "Rendezvous" is a working title. The protocol's substance is vendor-neutral and unowned.
